The Decentralized Libertarianism Manifesto

The Continuity Bureau

Read or fork the raw Markdown →

"The most dangerous phrase in any language is: someone else is handling it."

§ 0Declaration

We are not asking for anything.

That is the first thing you need to understand about this document, because it is the thing that distinguishes it from every petition, platform, and open letter you have ever skimmed and forgotten. Manifestos usually conclude with demands. Demands presume a recipient with the authority to grant them, which means every demand is also a confession: we believe you are in charge of us.

We don't.

The Continuity Bureau is a political movement for people in the United States and Canada who have noticed that centralized authority — in government, in money, in software, in speech — is not a law of nature. It is an architecture. Architectures have failure modes, and this one has been failing in production for decades while its operators mark the incidents as resolved.

We are not here to seize the architecture. We are here to make it optional.

§ 1Post-Mortem: What Centralization Actually Costs

Treat the last half-century as an incident report, because that is what it is.

Severity: ongoing. Root cause: single points of failure, deliberately installed, described as features.

A centralized system is one where the blast radius of a bad decision is everyone. One committee misprices credit and two nations inherit the recession. One platform adjusts a ranking function and a million small businesses discover their customers were never theirs. One agency classifies a document and a generation votes without the information the vote was supposedly about. One payment processor decides your industry is distasteful this quarter, and your legal business stops clearing.

None of these are hypotheticals. All of them have shipped.

The defenders of centralization will tell you these are bugs — regrettable, fixable, exceptional. They are wrong in the way that matters most: these are not bugs. They are the specified behavior of systems designed so that decisions flow to a summit and consequences flow to a base. The summit calls this efficiency. The base experiences it as living inside someone else's error budget.

Note what centralization does to information, because this is the part its operators genuinely do not understand. Every layer between a decision and its consequences deletes signal. The bureaucrat in the capital is not evil; the bureaucrat is blind, structurally, no matter how good the intentions or how large the staff. Prices, local knowledge, the thousand small facts of an actual community — these do not survive transmission up a hierarchy. The summit governs a simulation of the country and is perpetually surprised when the country diverges from it.

And note the accountability model, or rather its absence. When a distributed system fails, you know which node failed. When a centralized system fails, responsibility is sharded across so many committees that it asymptotically approaches zero. Nobody resigns. The budget increases. The failure is cited as proof that the failing institution needs more authority. This is the only perpetual motion machine humanity has ever successfully built.

The cost of centralization is not tyranny, usually. Tyranny is the dramatic failure mode and it makes for good dystopian fiction. The common failure mode is worse because it is survivable: mediocrity at continental scale, enforced uniformly, with no exit. Everything gets slightly worse forever, and you are told this is the price of order.

We decline to pay it.

§ 2The Principles

These are not aspirations. They are design constraints. A movement, like a protocol, is defined by what it refuses to accept as valid input.

  1. The individual is the base layer. Every family, firm, church, commune, city, and state is an abstraction built on top of individuals. Abstractions are useful. Abstractions are also optional. Nothing built on the base layer outranks it.
  2. Every centralizing abstraction must justify its existence continuously — or be forked. Not once, at founding, in a document under glass. Continuously. Authority is a service with an uptime requirement, and "we have always been in charge" is not a status page.
  3. Verify, don't trust. Institutions earn exactly the authority their proofs support. No proofs, no authority. An institution that answers "trust us" to a request for evidence has answered the question, just not the way it thinks.
  4. Subsidiarity is not a preference; it is an engineering principle. Every decision is made at the smallest scale competent to make it. The individual before the household, the household before the town, the town before the province or state, and the nation almost never. Escalation requires justification. De-escalation does not.
  5. Voluntary association is the only legitimate membership. Any affiliation you cannot leave is not a community. It is a containment structure with a newsletter.
  6. No walled gardens. Not in software, not in money, not in speech, not in governance. A garden you cannot exit with your data, your funds, your voice, and your dignity intact was never a garden. It was a terrarium, and you were the exhibit.
  7. Exit outranks voice. Voice is the right to complain to management. Exit is the right to stop having management. We support both. We are under no illusions about which one management actually respects.
  8. Open source is civic virtue. A rule you cannot read is not a rule; it is an ambush. This applies to code, to law, to budgets, to the algorithms that decide what you see. Closed systems governing public life are guilty until audited.
  9. Responsibility to the responsible. Respect, authority, and trust are extended to those who demonstrate them and withdrawn from those who don't — individually, by evidence, without regard to title, credential, or incumbency. We do not grade on a curve and we do not grade by category.
  10. Build > protest. A protest asks the incumbent to change. A working alternative makes the incumbent irrelevant. One of these has a track record.
  11. Competence is the only aristocracy we acknowledge, and it is not hereditary, not credentialed by default, and not transferable. It is demonstrated per task, forever.
  12. Entropy is on our side. Centralized systems require constant energy to maintain their exemptions from reality. We do not have to defeat them. We have to outlast them, and build the thing people walk to when the walls get tiresome. This is not patience. It is thermodynamics.

§ 3Verify, Don't Trust — What This Actually Means

"Trust the institutions" was, for a long time, reasonable advice, in the way that "trust the mainframe" was reasonable advice: there was no alternative, so the trust was not a choice, and calling it trust flattered everyone involved.

There are alternatives now. Cryptographic verification, open ledgers, reproducible builds, public audit trails, signed statements that cannot be quietly edited after the fact. The tools exist to replace "take our word for it" with "check for yourself" across most of public life. The interesting question is why institutions that claim to serve the public are so consistently allergic to adopting them.

The answer is not conspiracy. It is comfort. Verification is friction for the verified. An institution that has spent a century being believed experiences the request for proof as an insult, the way a landlord experiences a building inspector.

We are the inspector. We are unbothered by the reaction.

To be precise, because precision is the point: verify, don't trust does not mean trust nothing. It means trust is a cache, and caches need invalidation. Extend trust provisionally, verify continuously, and revoke on failed proof — without drama, without hatred, without a second chance being confused for a policy of infinite chances. An institution that passes its audits deserves its authority. Most have simply never been asked to sit the exam.

§ 4Subsidiarity: The Smallest Competent Scale

Here is a test you can run on any political question: what is the smallest group of people who both feel the consequences of this decision and possess the information to make it?

That group should make the decision. Full stop.

What you will find, running this test honestly, is that the answer is almost never "a continental government." The federal governments of the United States and Canada are currently making decisions about school curricula, small business licensing, what you may ingest, how you may build on your own land, and which words are acceptable — decisions whose consequences they will never feel and whose local context they cannot possibly hold.

This is not malice. It is scope creep, the oldest disease of any long-running system. Every organization expands to the limit of what it is permitted to touch, and no organization has ever been observed to voluntarily return authority to a lower layer. Deallocation, in politics, has to be forced.

Subsidiarity is how we force it, peacefully and permanently: by building the lower layers back into competence. A town that runs its own resilient services does not need to beg. A community with its own mutual-aid rails does not wait on a disaster agency's press conference. A person who can transact, publish, organize, and verify without permission is a person whose consent must actually be earned.

The summit's authority is, and has always been, a function of the base's dependence. We are addressing the dependence. The authority will adjust on its own schedule.

§ 5The Fork Is the Ballot

Open source gave political philosophy its most important gift in three centuries, and almost nobody in political philosophy noticed.

The gift is the fork.

In closed systems, disagreement has two outcomes: submission or destruction. You accept the maintainers' decision, or you burn the project down. Every civil war in history is a merge conflict resolved with the second option.

The fork introduces a third path: take the code and leave. Nobody is destroyed. Nobody submits. The disagreement is resolved by reality — both versions run, and the users decide, continuously, with their feet. The original maintainers keep everything they had except the thing they never legitimately owned: the ability to prevent alternatives.

We consider the fork the most civilized dispute-resolution mechanism ever devised, and we intend to apply it well beyond software. Communities can fork. Institutions can fork. Standards, currencies, curricula, and cities can fork. The right to fork is the right to exit made constructive: not merely leaving, but leaving with the source, and building the version that should have existed.

Incumbents call this fragmentation. They would. From the summit, every alternative looks like a defect in the landscape. From the base, it looks like a market.

§ 6What the Bureau Builds

Protocols, not petitions. Tools, not slogans. Specifically:

Reference implementations of self-sovereignty. Working, documented, open-source infrastructure for the functions people currently rent from gatekeepers: identity you hold, communication that cannot be silently intercepted or retroactively edited, records that survive the failure or displeasure of any single company or agency, local commerce rails that clear without asking a coastal risk department for its blessing.

Verification tooling for public institutions. If a budget, a voting record, a contract award, or an algorithmic decision affects the public, it should be inspectable by the public in machine-readable form — and where the institutions won't publish, we build the scrapers, the mirrors, and the diff logs that publish anyway, from public sources, within the law. Sunlight as a service.

Subsidiarity kits. Playbooks and software for communities that want to re-onshore their own competence: mutual aid coordination, local dispute resolution by voluntary agreement, resilient utilities, cooperative purchasing. The goal is boring and radical: towns that work whether or not the capital is having a functional decade.

Civic literacy for the verification age. Not "media literacy" as taught by media companies, which is fox-authored henhouse security. Actual epistemics: how to read a primary source, how to check a signature, how to distinguish a proof from a press release, how to notice when you are being asked to trust instead of shown.

The administrative machinery of a real political movement, built in the open, at the pace that honest paperwork actually takes. See the status section. We are not going to lie to you about where that stands, because a movement founded on verification that starts by inflating its own metrics would be a punchline, and we have standards.

Everything we build ships under open licenses. Everything we build works without our permission — including, especially, without ours. If the Bureau vanished tomorrow, the tools would keep running. That is not a risk we tolerate. It is the acceptance criterion.

§ 7What the Bureau Refuses

A movement is defined by its refusals more than its ambitions. Ours are absolute, and they are not concessions to respectability. They are load-bearing.

We refuse violence — actual, threatened, or winked at. Not because we are timid, but because violence is the incumbent's home turf and the centralizer's favorite gift. Every broken window builds a surveillance budget. Beyond the strategy, there is the arithmetic of dignity: we do not need to threaten anyone. Entropy is already on our side. Systems that require constant coercion to maintain themselves are paying interest on a loan that we are simply declining to co-sign. We can wait. We build while waiting.

We refuse hatred and dehumanization of any group. Our contempt is reserved for systems, architectures, and patterns of power — and it is fully allocated there, with none left over for categories of human beings. Anyone who arrives carrying a theory about which class of people is the real problem has misdiagnosed the incident and will be shown the door, which, in a voluntary association, is always unlocked in both directions.

We refuse cults of personality. Including — let us be explicit — around our own founder. A movement that cannot survive its founder's absence is not a movement; it is a fan club with a compliance department. Everything here is designed to be forkable, and that includes the leadership. If the Bureau's stewards ever fail their own audits, the correct response is the one this document teaches: take the source and build the better version. We will have deserved it.

We refuse election disinformation and fabricated claims. We will not tell you an election was stolen without proof, and we will not tell you our movement is larger, more official, or further along than it is. A verification movement gets exactly one credibility, non-refundable.

We refuse attacks on named individuals. Institutions are fair game. Incentive structures are fair game. Patterns are fair game. Persons are not targets. This is partly ethics and partly engineering: individuals are replaceable components of the systems we oppose, and burning effort on the component while the architecture stands is a rookie mistake.

We refuse coercive recruitment and retention. No pressure, no shame-based loyalty, no manufactured urgency. If our arguments require a sales funnel, they are not arguments.

We refuse token and coin promotion. This movement does not sell you an asset, and by its founder's own declaration it is post-coin. Decentralization is an architecture, not a ticker symbol, and the fastest way to discredit the former is to confuse it with the latter.

§ 8On Influence, and the Limits of It

Honesty about intellectual lineage is a verification practice too, so: this movement's individualism is influenced by, among other traditions, the LaVeyan current — its insistence on the individual as sovereign, its allergy to herd conformity, its principle that respect and responsibility flow to those who demonstrate them rather than to those who demand them. We found those postures useful. We built on them in our own words, for our own purposes.

Influence is not affiliation. The Continuity Bureau has no connection to, endorsement from, or affiliation with the Church of Satan or any religious organization, and claims nothing of the sort. We are not a religion. We do not do metaphysics. We do infrastructure. Our position on the supernatural is the same as our position on every other unverifiable authority: interesting claim; show the proofs; we'll be over here building either way.

The same disclosure applies to every tradition we have drawn from — the classical liberals, the free-software movement, the cypherpunks, the subsidiarity theorists. We inherited good ideas from all of them and marching orders from none of them. That is what inheritance is supposed to look like.

§ 9Membership: A Voluntary Association, Meaning It

Membership in the Continuity Bureau is voluntary in both directions, which is rarer than it sounds.

You join by deciding you have joined and acting like it: building, verifying, teaching, running the tools, forking what fails you. There is no oath, because oaths are for institutions that expect your future self to want out. There is no fee at the philosophical layer, because principles that require a paywall are merchandise. There will be ordinary administrative membership as the formal organizations stand up — see the status section — and it will be exactly as exciting as membership paperwork should be, which is not at all.

You leave by leaving. No exit interview, no shunning, no dark pattern. The door works. We are, to our knowledge, one of the few political movements confident enough in the product to publish the cancellation flow on page one.

What we ask of members is the only thing we ask of institutions: demonstrate responsibility and receive it. Show up competent, be trusted with more. Fail, and be trusted with less — not exiled, just accurately rated. It is remarkable how much organizational pathology evaporates when status is indexed to demonstrated competence instead of tenure, volume, or loyalty.

§ 10Two Countries, One Failure Mode

This movement spans the Canada–US border because the incident does.

The accents differ; the architecture is identical. Ottawa and Washington are separate installations of the same design pattern: authority pooled at the summit, competence assumed at the summit, consequences delivered to the base, and a national mythology in each case explaining why this particular centralization is the exceptional, benevolent kind. Canadians are told their federal concentration is politeness. Americans are told theirs is greatness. It is the same load-bearing wall with two coats of paint.

And in both countries, the same truth holds one layer down: the places actually work. The towns, the neighborhoods, the shops, the volunteer fire halls, the maker spaces, the mutual-aid spreadsheets that outperform billion-dollar agencies in every disaster — the base layer is fine. Better than fine. It is the most competent part of both nations, and it has been treated as a rounding error by summits that could not run a lemonade stand at its level of reliability.

Founded from Toronto, addressed to both countries: subsidiarity does not stop at borders because it was never about borders. It is about scale, and the right scale for most of life is the one where you know the people involved.

We will pursue formal political standing in both countries, through each one's actual legal machinery, honestly and in the open. Which brings us to the part most manifestos omit.

§ 11Status: Founding Phase — Read This Part

A movement built on verify, don't trust owes you its own audit. Here it is, current as of publication.

The Continuity Bureau is in its founding phase. It is not, at this time, a registered political party in any state of the United States. It is not, at this time, a registered political party with Elections Canada or any provincial elections authority. No candidates. No ballot lines. No electoral track record. Registration in relevant jurisdictions is administrative work in progress, and it will take the time honest paperwork takes.

We state this plainly because the alternative — implying official standing we do not have — is precisely the species of lie this movement exists to make unaffordable. Incumbents inflate their legitimacy as a matter of routine. We are structurally forbidden from it: our entire pitch is that claims should match proofs, and this claim, today, proofs out to founding phase, building in public, papers pending.

If you require a finished institution before you will consider an idea, come back later; we will be further along and the door will still work. If you can read a design document and recognize a sound architecture before the building is up — you are the base layer this was written for.

We are not asking for anything. We are building it. You are welcome to help, welcome to watch, and welcome to fork.
